Community Cadres: The Backbone of Rural Transformation

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

Source: PIB

Why in News?

A vast network of trained grassroots workers is strengthening last-mile delivery of government schemes by connecting rural communities with essential services related to livelihoods, health, nutrition, sanitation, housing, water security and financial inclusion .

How are Community Cadres Powering Last-Mile Delivery?

  • About Community Cadres: Community cadres act as trained grassroots workers who bridge the gap between government institutions and rural households by enabling awareness, service delivery, scheme access and behavioural change.
  • Role:
    • Multi-Dimensional Approach: Household-level convergence connects rural families with multiple development dimensions, including livelihoods, health, sanitation and financial inclusion .
    • Expanding Roles of Frontline Workers: Cadres increasingly perform cross-sectoral roles; for example, ASHAs support maternal and child health along with nutrition awareness, sanitation practices and response to violence against women .
    • Social Inclusion through Gender CRPs: Under NRLM , Gender CRPs address issues such as gender-based violence, social exclusion and inequality , promoting equitable development.
    • Last-Mile Enablers of Convergence: Frontline cadres convert coordination between schemes into tangible improvements in household well-being by ensuring integrated service delivery at the community level.
  • Extensive Grassroots Network: Over 9 lakh Community Resource Persons (CRPs) , 10.40 lakh ASHAs , 10.58 lakh Anganwadi Workers (AWWs), and Auxiliary Nurse Midwives (ANMs) along with other frontline workers, support rural service delivery across multiple sectors.
  • Sectoral Support: Nearly 4.08 lakh livelihood CRPs such as Krishi Sakhis, Pashu Sakhis, Matsya Sakhis and Van Sakhis support agriculture and allied activities, while other CRPs promote banking, insurance, financial literacy, gender awareness and community development .

Capacity Building for Community Cadres

  • Institutionalised Training Approach: Capacity building of community cadres is a continuous process involving induction training, thematic modules, refresher courses, field mentoring and on-site handholding rather than a one-time exercise.
  • NRLM Cadre Ecosystem: Under the National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM) , cadres such as Krishi Sakhis, Pashu Sakhis, Matsya Sakhis and CRP-EPs receive classroom-based and field-based training in agriculture, allied sectors and community mobilisation .
  • Health and Nutrition: ASHAs undergo phased training spread over 23 days , supported by field tools and regular skill reinforcement to strengthen community healthcare delivery.
  • Sanitation: Swachhagrahis are trained to promote behaviour change, hygiene practices and sustained sanitation outcomes under sanitation programmes.
  • Water Security: VWSC members and Jal Sakhis receive training in village water planning, water quality monitoring and operation & maintenance of water systems .
  • Rural Housing: Awaas Mitras are trained in beneficiary identification, construction monitoring and convergence with other government schemes .
  • Common Institutional Model: Across sectors, community cadres are strengthened through continuous capacity building, decentralised knowledge transfer and sustained community presence , enabling effective last-mile service delivery.

How are Community Cadres Enabling Rural Livelihood Transformation?

  • DAY-NRLM as the Foundation: The Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana–National Rural Livelihoods Mission (DAY-NRLM) promotes community-led rural development by enabling access to livelihood opportunities, financial services, social development interventions and government schemes at the local level.
  • Women-led Community Institutions: DAY-NRLM has mobilised over 10 crore rural households into around 92 lakh Self-Help Groups (SHGs) , supported by Village Organisations and Cluster-Level Federations .
  • Role of Community Resource Persons (CRPs): More than 9 lakh CRPs support rural communities across agriculture, livestock, fisheries, enterprise promotion, banking, financial literacy, insurance and social development .
  • Farm and Non-Farm Livelihood Support: CRPs bridge gaps in knowledge, services, credit and market access , helping rural households improve productivity and create sustainable livelihood opportunities across both farm and non-farm sectors.
  • Women as Change Agents: Cadres such as Krishi Sakhis , Pashu Sakhis , Matsya Sakhis and Community Resource Persons for Enterprise Promotion (CRP-EP) enable rural women to transition from programme beneficiaries to trained service providers, community leaders and local change agents .
    • These community cadres are drawn from local communities and receive training, field-based handholding and continuous capacity building in their respective areas.
  • Farm Livelihood Support: Livelihood CRPs help SHG members and rural households adopt improved agricultural practices, strengthen livestock management, enhance productivity and access livelihood services . As of June 2025, over 4.62 crore Mahila Kisans were covered under farm livelihood interventions with support from 3 lakh+ trained livelihood CRPs .
  • Rural Entrepreneurship through SVEP: Under the Start-up Village Entrepreneurship Programme (SVEP) , 4.32 lakh enterprises have been supported (June 2026), creating an institutional ecosystem for rural enterprises.
  • Future Expansion: The National Campaign on Entrepreneurship aims to train 50,000 CRPs on enterprise promotion and provide enterprise development training to 50 lakh SHG members , further strengthening rural entrepreneurship.

How are Community Cadres Driving Financial Inclusion?

  • BC Sakhis and Bank Sakhis: Under DAY-NRLM , trained women community cadres act as grassroots financial intermediaries, providing doorstep banking and credit facilitation services in rural areas.
  • Doorstep Banking Services: Over 1.48 lakh BC Sakhis provide services such as bank account opening, deposits, withdrawals, fund transfers and balance enquiries , improving access to digital financial services.
  • SHG Credit Linkage: More than 50,548 Bank Sakhis facilitate credit access for Self-Help Groups (SHGs), helping SHGs access ₹13.41 lakh crore in bank credit since 2013-14 .
  • Financial Literacy Promotion: Around 56,727 Financial Literacy CRPs conduct village-level awareness programmes on savings, credit, insurance, pensions, financial planning and enterprise financing .
  • Women’s Financial Empowerment: During 2025-26 , over 3.81 crore women SHG members received financial literacy training, strengthening informed financial decision-making and promoting inclusive rural finance.

How are Community Cadres Supporting Social Development?

Health & Nutrition

  • Role of ASHAs: They promote healthcare awareness, support national health programmes and respond to local health needs through community-based interventions.
  • Nutrition and Behaviour Change: AWWs identify Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) and Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM) cases, provide supplementary nutrition and promote awareness on child feeding, breastfeeding, hygiene and sanitation .
  • Poshan Bhi Padhai Bhi Initiative: Focuses on improving ECCE quality through better Anganwadi infrastructure, play-based learning materials and capacity building of AWWs.

Sanitation and Hygiene

  • Swachhagrahis as Community Motivators: Under Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen) , Swachhagrahis promote sanitation awareness, hygiene practices and sustainable behaviour change in rural areas.
  • Inclusive Community Workforce: Swachhagrahis include ASHA workers, ANMs, AWWs, water linemen, pump operators and village volunteers , ensuring wider community participation.

Water Access through Local Cadres

  • Community-led Water Governance: Under the Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM) and the framework of the 73rd Constitutional Amendment , Gram Panchayats and Village Water & Sanitation Committees (VWSCs)/Pani Samitis enable village-level planning, implementation and maintenance of water supply systems.
  • Inclusive Participation: VWSCs are formed through the Gram Sabha and ensure at least 50% representation of women , along with participation of Panchayat members, SC/ST representatives and vulnerable households .
  • Role of VWSCs: Over 5.33 lakh VWSCs/Pani Samitis manage village water security by handling fund management, budgeting, user charges, water system maintenance and monitoring of water quality and health indicators .
  • Nal Jal Mitras: The Nal Jal Mitra Programme strengthens local technical capacity by training community members in plumbing, electrical work and pump operation for maintenance and minor repairs of rural water supply systems.

Homes Built with Community Support

  • Role of Awaas Mitras: Under Pradhan Mantri Awaas Yojana-Gramin (PMAY-G) , Awaas Mitras act as field-level facilitators supporting beneficiary identification, documentation, construction monitoring and scheme implementation .
  • Continuous Beneficiary Support: Each sanctioned house is linked with village-level functionaries such as Awaas Mitras, Awaas Sahayaks, Gram Rozgar Sahayaks or SHG members to ensure timely completion.
  • Housing Target: Through this community-supported approach, PMAY-G aims to construct 4.95 crore houses by March 2029 , improving access to safe and secure rural housing.

Conclusion

Community cadres represent the human infrastructure of rural transformation . By connecting government programmes with households, they strengthen access, awareness, capacity building and service delivery . From livelihoods and financial inclusion to health, sanitation, water and housing, these grassroots workers are transforming rural development from a scheme-based approach into a community-driven model of inclusive growth .

International Conference on The Future of Environment and Climate Dynamics

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

Source: AIR

Why in News?

The Prime Minister of India inaugurated the two-day International Conference on "The Future of Environment and Climate Dynamics" in New Delhi. The event was organized by the National Green Tribunal (NGT).

What are the Key Highlights of the Conference?

  • About: The Conference brings together jurists, experts, policymakers and stakeholders from 17 countries , along with United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) and Asian Development Bank (ADB) .
    • It focuses on knowledge exchange and best practices in judicial, scientific and policy domains.
    • It promotes collaborative action to address global environmental and climate challenges .
  • Core Themes: It promoted dialogues on the intricate relationship between the environment, climate change, development, and environmental justice.

Major Highlights of the PM’s Address

  • Per Capita Carbon Emissions & Equity: India’s per capita greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions remain less than half the global average, whereas developed nations emit up to six times more per capita.
    • According to the UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2024 , India’s per capita GHG emissions were 2.9 tCO₂e in 2022 , compared with the global average of 6.6 tCO₂e per person per year .
    • The Prime Minister reiterated the principle of Common But Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR–RC) , emphasizing that developing nations must not be unfairly penalized for historical emissions.
  • Early Achievement of COP21 Paris NDCs: India is the only G20 nation to fulfill its original Paris Agreement (COP21) Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) well ahead of the 2030 deadline (surpassing the 50% non-fossil fuel electric capacity milestone and drastically slashing carbon intensity).
  • Pioneering Global Green Alliances: Emphasized that India continues to lead multilateral climate diplomacy through homegrown global frameworks:
    • International Solar Alliance (ISA) and One Sun, One World, One Grid (OSOWOG)
    • Global Biofuels Alliance (GBA)
    • International Big Cat Alliance (IBCA)
    • Mission LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment) for mass behavioural change
  • Exponential Clean Energy Expansion: Non-fossil energy capacity has expanded by nearly 400% over the last 12 years.
    • Solar capacity grew exponentially from ~2 GW (2014) to over 160 GW (2026), alongside a threefold surge in wind energy and a strategic push for nuclear power.
  • Judicial Accessibility for the Environment: Inaugurated NGT Mobile App to democratize legal remedies, expedite dispute resolution, and bolster transparent environmental governance.

National Green Tribunal (NGT)

  • About: The National Green Tribunal (NGT) is a specialized statutory body established under the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, to provide swift and effective resolution of multi-disciplinary environmental disputes.
  • Procedural Flexibility: As per Section 19 of the NGT Act, the tribunal is not bound by the rigid procedures laid down in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 or the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 . Instead, it has the autonomy to regulate its own procedure.
  • Judicial Framework: Operates on the principles of natural justice rather than the rigid Civil Procedure Code. NGT aims to dispose of cases within 6 months.
  • Tribunal Composition: Headed by a Chairperson (a retired Supreme Court Judge or Chief Justice of a High Court ), supported by 10 to 20 Judicial Members and 10 to 20 Expert Members possessing specialized environmental or scientific credentials.
  • Powers and Enforcement: Functions with the authority of a Civil Court and exercises suo motu powers (initiating cases independently without a formal complaint).
    • The Supreme Court of India, in the landmark case of Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai v. Ankita Sinha (2021), upheld this suo motu jurisdiction, recognizing the NGT as a sui generis (unique) institution meant to proactively address environmental degradation.
    • Furthermore, it has the mandate to impose strict penalties, order environmental restitution, and award compensation to victims under the Polluter Pays Principle and the Precautionary Principle.
  • Appellate Mechanism: Decisions made by the NGT are legally binding, though an appeal can be filed directly before the Supreme Court of India within 90 days .
  • Significance: Enforces the fundamental right to a healthy environment under Article 21 , ensures stringent compliance with Environment Impact Assessments (EIA) , and significantly reduces the environmental litigation burden on regular courts.

Tulu Accorded 2nd Official Language Status in Karnataka

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source: TH

The Karnataka government has granted Tulu the status of the second additional administrative language in the State. The status will apply specifically to the coastal districts of Dakshina Kannada and Udupi , where Tulu is widely spoken.

  • The government will allocate Rs 82 lakh annually for translation, training and other activities related to the use of Tulu.
  • Background: The decision implements the recommendations of a committee headed by educationist M. Mohan Alva . This committee was appointed in 2023 to study the state of Tuluva culture and language and recommend pathways for its official recognition.
  • Constitutional Provisions: Under Article 345 of the Indian Constitution , a State Legislature is empowered to formally adopt Hindi or any regional language in use within the state as its official language for administrative purposes.
    • Article 347 grants the President of India the authority to direct the official recognition of a language across an entire state or a specific district if a substantial proportion of the population demands it.
    • At the national level, the Eighth Schedule of the Indian Constitution currently recognizes 22 languages , while Tulu remains outside it despite demands for its inclusion and greater recognition .

Tulu Language

  • Linguistic Lineage: Tulu is a highly prominent Dravidian language, often counted among the 'Pancha-Dravida' (Proto-Dravidian) languages, alongside Tamil, Kannada, Telugu, and Malayalam.
    • To record its rich literary and religious heritage, the region developed its own indigenous writing system known as the Tulu script (or Tigalari). A Southern Brahmic script that evolved from the medieval Grantha script .
  • Geographical Spread: It is widely spoken in the 'Tulunadu' region , which predominantly comprises the coastal districts of Karnataka (the Karavali region) and parts of the Kasaragod district in northern Kerala.
  • Cultural Significance: Tulu possesses a rich oral tradition, deeply intertwined with local folklore, theatre (such as Yakshagana ) , rituals like Bhuta Kola , and ancient epic poetry.
  • Historical Context: Prior to the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 , Tulu-speaking regions were under the Madras Presidency before being merged into the unified Mysore state (now Karnataka).

India-Pakistan Naval Collision in the North Arabian Sea

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source: TH

A diplomatic and maritime dispute erupted after the Pakistani offshore patrol vessel PNS Hunain collided with the Indian guided-missile destroyer INS Kolkata in the North Arabian Sea.

  • INS Kolkata (India): The lead ship of India's Project 15A Kolkata-class guided-missile destroyers , built by Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders. It displaces 7,400 tonnes and is equipped with the BrahMos supersonic cruise missile and advanced anti-submarine capabilities.
  • PNS Hunain (Pakistan): An Offshore Patrol Vessel (OPV) commissioned in July 2024 and built in Romania, designed primarily for maritime security and electronic warfare.
  • Key Bilateral Agreement: India alleged a violation of Article 10 of the 1991 India-Pakistan Agreement on Advance Notice of Military Exercises, Manoeuvres and Troop Movements.
    • This specific provision mandates that naval ships from both nations maintain a separation of three nautical miles (approx. 5.6 km) in international waters to avoid accidents.
    • India also stated that the Pakistani vessel violated the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs) , adopted under the International Maritime Organization (IMO) as the universal “rules of the road” for preventing collisions on the high seas and other connected navigable waters.
  • Historical Precedent: In 2011 , a similar collision occurred between the Pakistani warship PNS Babur and the Indian frigate INS Godavari in the Gulf of Aden.
  • Arabian Sea: The Arabian Sea is the northwestern arm of the Indian Ocean, spanning ~3.86 million sq. km with an average depth of 2,734 metres.
    • Boundaries : India (east), Pakistan & Iran (north), Arabian Peninsula (west), and Indian Ocean (south).
    • Significant Islands: Houses key island territories including Socotra (administered by Yemen), the Kuria Muria Islands (Oman), and India's Lakshadweep Union Territory (a cluster of coral atolls comprising the Laccadive, Minicoy, and Amindivi islands).
    • Critical Maritime Chokepoints:
      • Northwest Connection: Links to the Persian Gulf via the Gulf of Oman through the highly strategic Strait of Hormuz.
      • Western Connection: Links to the Red Sea via the Gulf of Aden through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait.
    • Strategic Ports & Geopolitics: Anchors key deep-sea nodes, notably Gwadar Port (Pakistan/CPEC) and Chabahar Port (Iran/INSTC).
    • Primary Drainage Systems: The sea receives massive freshwater and sediment discharge primarily from the Indus River in the north and the Narmada River from the Indian subcontinent.

Exercise Nomadic Elephant 2026

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source: PIB

Recently, the 18th edition of Exercise NOMADIC ELEPHANT , an annual India–Mongolia joint military exercise , commenced at the Foreign Training Node, Pithoragarh, Uttarakhand .

  • About: It is a platoon-level bilateral exercise conducted alternately between India and Mongolia , with the previous edition held at Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia, in May–June 2025.
  • Objective: The exercise aims to enhance the joint military capability of both armies to conduct Counter-Insurgency Operations (CI/CT) in semi-urban and mountainous terrain under a United Nations mandate .
  • Participants: The exercise involves 45 personnel each from the Indian Army and the Mongolian Armed Forces .
  • Training Focus: The exercise includes Cordon and Search Operations , Room Intervention Drills , Special Heliborne Operations , Cyber Security Training , Drone and Counter-Drone Operations and Exchange of best practices and operational drills .
  • Significance: The exercise strengthens defence cooperation, mutual trust and interoperability between India and Mongolia while enhancing preparedness for joint operations and peacekeeping missions.
  • Other Exercises: India also actively takes part in Exercise Khaan Quest , a multinational peacekeeping exercise organized by Mongolia.
