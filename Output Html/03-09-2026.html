26th Shanghai Cooperation Organisation Summit

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

For Prelims: Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) , Collective Security Treaty Organization , African Union , China-Pakistan Economic Corridor , Belt and Road Initiative , Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS)

For Mains: Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO): Evolution, objectives, structure and significance, India's role in the SCO and its relevance to India's strategic autonomy, Role of international and regional organisations in promoting a multipolar global order.

Source: AIR

Why in News?

The 26 th Meeting of the Council of Heads of State (CHS) of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) and the inaugural “SCO Plus” meeting were held in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan .

  • Marking the 25 th anniversary of the SCO, the summit was chaired by Kyrgyzstan under the theme , "25 Years of the SCO: Together for Sustainable Peace, Development and Prosperity."

Summary

  • The 26th SCO Summit in Bishkek strengthened cooperation in regional security, counter-terrorism, connectivity, technology, environment and economic development , while adopting several institutional and cooperation measures.
  • For India , the SCO remains an important platform to advance strategic autonomy, Central Asian connectivity, counter-terrorism and multi-alignment , while promoting a more multipolar world order .

What are the Key Outcomes of the 26 th SCO Summit in Kyrgyzstan?

  • Bishkek Declaration: The SCO leaders signed and issued the Bishkek Declaration , reaffirming cooperation on regional peace, security, development and the organisation's future direction.
  • SCO Charter Amendments: The leaders approved the Protocol on Amendments and Additions to the SCO Charter , aimed at updating the organisation's institutional framework.
    • It allows newer members, such as India, to actively participate in shaping the organization's future structural and legal framework.
  • Universal Security Centre: The SCO leaders approved the regulations to establish the Universal Centre for Countering Challenges and Threats to the Security of SCO Member States.
    • This aims to enhance intelligence sharing against terrorism, extremism, and organized crime.
  • SCO Anti-Drug Centre: The leaders approved the regulations on the Executive Committee of the SCO Anti-Drug Centre and adopted programmes on combating drug addiction and countering the spread of synthetic drugs and their precursors up to 2030 .
  • Economic & Green Corridors: Leaders signed an action plan for the development of ports and logistics centers across member states (2026–2030) and established the framework for the SCO “Green Technology Corridor” .
  • Global Institutional Partnerships: The summit approved the SCO–CSTO–CIS Roadmap for trilateral cooperation among the SCO, Collective Security Treaty Organization (CSTO), and Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS).
    • It also decided to sign a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) between the SCO Secretariat and the African Union Commission, further expanding the SCO’s engagement with regional organisations.
  • International Information Security: The SCO adopted a Plan of Cooperation on Ensuring International Information Security for 2026–2028 .
  • Biodiversity: The leaders adopted an initiative on the Conservation and Sustainable Management of Biodiversity in SCO Member States for 2026–2031 , along with a joint action plan.
  • Pakistan's Chairmanship: The rotating SCO chairmanship for 2026–2027 was handed over to Pakistan , which announced the theme “Turning Vision into Action: Connectivity, Innovation and Shared Prosperity” for its tenure.
    • Lahore was also designated as the SCO Tourism and Cultural Capital for 2026–2027 .

India at 26th SCO Summit

  • Three Pillars of Engagement: India’s Prime Minister outlined three core pillars for the SCO’s future: Security, Connectivity, and Opportunity .
  • Dismantling the Terror Ecosystem: India strongly called for a unified approach to counter-terrorism , urging member states to dismantle the financing and safe havens of terrorism without employing "double standards" .
    • Addressing global geopolitical conflicts, India reiterated its steadfast position that conflicts cannot be resolved on the battlefield ; dialogue and diplomacy are the only way forward.
  • Sovereignty in Connectivity: India stressed that all connectivity projects must respect the sovereignty and territorial integrity of nations (a clear, a principled, indirect critique of China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC)).
  • Indian-Led Initiatives:
    • Youth and Innovation: The SCO endorsed India’s initiatives to strengthen scientific and technical cooperation through the SCO Young Scientists Forum and SCO Young Authors Conference .
    • Civilisational Dialogue: Member states welcomed India’s initiative to host the first SCO Civilisation Dialogue Forum in 2026 .
    • Language Reform: During India’s 2022–23 SCO Presidency , India proposed adding English as an official and working language. The 26th SCO Summit added English alongside Russian and Chinese .

"SCO Plus" Format

  • About: The "SCO Plus" is an expanded multilateral dialogue mechanism that includes non-member states and international organisations to discuss global challenges and multipolarity.
  • Theme for 2026: “The UN as a Key Element of a Just World Order: The Contribution of the SCO Plus States to the Formation of a Multipolar World.”

Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)

  • About: The SCO is a permanent intergovernmental international organization, created in 2001 in Shanghai (China). It was preceded by the Shanghai Five (China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and Tajikistan) mechanism.
  • Significance: With the membership of 10 countries, the SCO represents a vast geopolitical space, accounting for approximately 25% of the global GDP and over 40% of the world's population.
  • Member States: China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, India, Pakistan, Iran, and Belarus (the newest member).
  • Observer States: Afghanistan and Mongolia.
  • Dialogue Partners: Azerbaijan, Armenia, Bahrain, Egypt, Cambodia, Qatar, Kuwait, Laos, Maldives, Myanmar, Nepal, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Türkiye and Sri Lanka.
  • Secretariat: Beijing, China.
  • Core Objectives: To strengthen mutual trust, maintain regional peace, security, and stability, and jointly address new challenges—primarily the "Three Evils" of Terrorism, Separatism, and Extremism .
  • Structure:
    • Heads of State Council (HSC): The supreme decision-making body of the SCO.
    • Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS): Headquartered in Tashkent (Uzbekistan), RATS is a permanent organ of the SCO that coordinates efforts of member states against terrorism, separatism, and extremism.

Why is the SCO Significant for India?

  • Security and Counter-Terrorism: Through the RATS mechanism , India gains access to intelligence sharing and joint counter-terrorism exercises, which is crucial for securing its borders and combatting cross-border terrorism emanating from its neighborhood.
  • Connect Central Asia Policy: The SCO provides a direct diplomatic and economic gateway to the resource-rich Central Asian Republics (CARs), a region where India lacks direct land access.
    • Central Asian nations (like Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan) are rich in hydrocarbons and uranium. Active participation in the SCO allows India to secure its long-term energy needs.
  • Balancing Strategic Autonomy: Engaging in the SCO (which includes China and Russia) while simultaneously being a core member of the Quad (with the US, Japan, and Australia) allows India to successfully exercise its policy of multi-alignment and strategic autonomy in a multipolar world.

What are the Key Challenges Facing the SCO and Measures to Address Them?

Area

Key Challenge for the SCO

Way Forward

Decision-Making

The SCO works largely through consensus . India-Pakistan tensions and India-China border disputes can therefore make it difficult to reach common decisions.

Develop confidence-building and dispute-management mechanisms within the SCO so that bilateral tensions do not block cooperation.

Geopolitical Balance

The growing influence of China and Russia , along with the inclusion of Iran and Belarus, creates a perception of the SCO as an anti-Western grouping . This can conflict with India's policy of strategic autonomy .

Preserve the SCO as a non-bloc and inclusive platform , rather than allowing it to become aligned against any particular power or group.

Terrorism & Security

The SCO's RATS mainly facilitates coordination and intelligence sharing but has limited capacity for enforcing collective action . Differences among members can also lead to inconsistent approaches towards terrorism.

Strengthen RATS through better intelligence sharing, common databases, coordinated action against terror financing and stronger implementation mechanisms.

Economic Cooperation

China's much larger economy gives it considerable influence within the SCO. Smaller economies may face trade imbalances and excessive dependence on Chinese capital and markets.

Promote balanced economic cooperation , including equitable financing mechanisms and greater trade among all member states.

Connectivity

Eurasian connectivity is divided among competing initiatives such as China's BRI, India's INSTC and Trans-Caspian routes . Different customs procedures and geopolitical concerns further slow connectivity.

Develop interoperable transport corridors , harmonise customs procedures and promote connectivity projects that respect the sovereignty of member states .

Conclusion

The SCO can play a meaningful role in shaping a multipolar and just world order only if it moves beyond internal bilateral disputes, addresses terrorism through consistent standards, and focuses on practical cooperation. For India, the SCO remains a vital Eurasian platform to preserve strategic autonomy , strengthen connectivity with Central Asia, and advance its economic and security interests.

Interim Government of 1946

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

Source: TH

Why in News?

2 nd September marks the anniversary of the formation of the Interim Government of India in 1946 , headed by Jawaharlal Nehru . It was the first institutional framework where Indian leaders took executive charge of British India , marking a pivotal milestone in the formal transfer of power leading to independence on 15 th August 1947.

What was the Interim Government of 1946?

  • Background and Genesis: Following World War II , economic exhaustion and mutinies (such as the Royal Indian Navy Mutiny of 1946 ) compelled the British government under Clement Attlee to accelerate the transfer of power.
  • Cabinet Mission Plan (1946): Dispatched in March 1946, the Cabinet Mission was headed by Lord Pethick-Lawrence (Secretary of State for India), with Sir Stafford Cripps (President of the Board of Trade) and A.V. Alexander (First Lord of the Admiralty) as its other members.
    • The Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) rejected the demand for Pakistan and proposed a three-tier federal structure (Union, Section and Provincial levels) , with provinces grouped into three sections: Section A (Hindu-majority provinces), Section B (Muslim-majority provinces), and Section C (Bengal and Assam) .
    • It provided for a 389-member Constituent Assembly , and the Plan also provided for the lapse of British paramountcy over princely states and the formation of an Interim Government during the transition to independence.
  • Interim Government: The Interim Government was a provisional administration formed to manage executive functions and assist in the transition from British colonial rule to independence.
    • Cabinet System: Intended to operate as a cabinet government , rather than merely a collection of departmental heads under the Viceroy.
    • Key Objectives: Prepare India for independence, coordinate the transfer of power, and facilitate the functioning of the Constituent Assembly.
  • Legal Framework: It functioned under the existing Government of India Act, 1919 , through the restructured Viceroy’s Executive Council . The Viceroy remained the formal constitutional head , while Indian leaders were expected to exercise real executive authority .
  • Leadership: The Viceroy (Lord Wavell) remained the ex-officio President of the Executive Council.
    • Jawaharlal Nehru was appointed as the Vice-President of the Executive Council , effectively acting as the de facto Prime Minister and head of internal administration.
  • Direct Action Day: While the Indian National Congress agreed to join, negotiations broke down over grouping clauses and communal representation.
    • The All-India Muslim League withdrew its acceptance and launched 'Direct Action Day' (16 th August 1946) , triggering widespread communal riots, notably the Great Calcutta Killings.
    • Despite the turmoil and the Muslim League's initial boycott, Viceroy Wavell invited Jawaharlal Nehru to form the administration, which officially took office on 2 nd September 1946.
  • Entry of Muslim League: Realizing that staying out marginalized its influence, the Muslim League joined the Interim Government on 26 th October 1946, without withdrawing its rejection of the Cabinet Mission Plan or renouncing Direct Action.
    • Three Congress members resigned to accommodate five League nominees. Liaquat Ali Khan was appointed as the Finance Member, where he used financial powers to obstruct several Congress-led initiatives.
  • Significance of the Interim Government:
    • Independent Foreign Policy: Nehru laid the foundations for non-alignment and independent diplomacy.
    • Princely States: Addressed relations with princely states and laid the groundwork for their future integration into the Indian Union , with a coordinated mechanism considered necessary during the transition.
    • Indianisation of Administration: Began transferring control of the civil services, police and armed forces to Indian leaders.
    • Governing Experience: Indian leaders gained experience in administration, food management and developmental planning .
    • Realization of the Inevitability of Partition: The paralysis within the Interim Government convinced leaders like Sardar Patel and Jawaharlal Nehru that an unworkable coalition at the Centre would lead to continuous civil strife, paving the way for the acceptance of the Lord Mountbatten's Partition Plan (3 rd June 1947) .

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

It officially took office on 2 September 1946 , with Jawaharlal Nehru as Vice-President of the Viceroy’s Executive Council .

2. What was the role of the Cabinet Mission Plan in the formation of the Interim Government?
The Cabinet Mission Plan (1946) proposed an Interim Government supported by major political parties and the formation of a Constituent Assembly .

3. When did the Muslim League join the Interim Government?

4. Why was the Interim Government significant for India’s independence?

5. How did the Interim Government contribute to Partition?
Persistent Congress–Muslim League deadlock and administrative paralysis strengthened the view among leaders that a united central government was becoming unworkable, contributing to acceptance of the Mountbatten Plan, 1947 .

Namami Gange Programme

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

Source: PIB

Why in News?

The Government has highlighted the role of Jan Ganga , the public participation pillar of the Namami Gange Programme , in transforming river conservation from a government-led activity into a people-driven movement .

What are the Key Facts About the Namami Gange Programme (NGP)?

  • About: The Namami Gange Programme (NGP) was launched in 2014 as a Flagship Integrated Conservation Mission for the effective abatement of pollution, conservation, and rejuvenation of the National River Ganga and its tributaries .
  • Integrated Approach: The programme focuses not only on cleaning the river but also on ecological restoration, biodiversity conservation, sustainable management, and improving the overall health of the river ecosystem .
  • Implementation Framework: The programme follows a phased approach through Entry-Level Activities (immediate visible impact), Medium-Term Activities, and Long-Term Activities for comprehensive river rejuvenation.
  • Community-Centric Approach: Recognising that long-term river conservation requires public participation, NGP promotes Jan Ganga , based on the principle of Jan Bhagidari , to transform river conservation from a government initiative into a people-driven movement .
  • Major Focus Areas: The programme covers activities such as sewage treatment, riverfront development, afforestation, scientific monitoring, and public awareness campaigns .

Key Achievements Under Namami Gange Programme

  • Project Implementation: Under NGP, 524 projects have been sanctioned , out of which 355 projects have been completed , covering river rejuvenation, pollution control, and ecological restoration.
  • Biodiversity Conservation: Scientific conservation initiatives have strengthened protection of aquatic species such as Gangetic Dolphin , Gharial , turtles , Hilsa, and otters through restoration, rescue, and rehabilitation programmes.
  • Ecosystem Restoration: Forestry interventions along the Ganga basin have resulted in afforestation of around 33,024 hectares , improving riverine ecology and biodiversity.
  • River Health Improvement: CPCB monitoring shows improvement in water quality, with pH and Dissolved Oxygen (DO) levels meeting bathing criteria across monitored locations of the Ganga .

How does Community Participation under the Namami Gange Programme Promote River Conservation?

  • Jan Ganga Initiative: Jan Ganga is the public participation pillar of the Namami Gange Programme , based on the principle of Jan Bhagidari (people’s participation) . It aims to transform river conservation from a government-led activity into a community-driven movement by promoting awareness, participation, and ownership.
  • River Cleanliness Drives: Initiatives like Ganga Swachhta Pakhwada (16–31 March) encourage students, volunteers, community groups, and stakeholders across Ganga basin states to participate in cleanliness activities, making river conservation a regular civic responsibility.
  • Plantation and Riverbank Restoration: Plantation campaigns such as “ Ek Ped Maa Ke Naam ” promote community involvement in tree plantation, helping in riverbank stabilisation, reducing soil erosion, and restoring riverine ecosystems .
  • Youth Engagement: University Ganga Clubs, eco-clubs, and educational institutions involve youth through lectures, field visits, volunteering, competitions, and awareness programmes, creating long-term environmental responsibility.
  • Cultural and Awareness Programmes: Ganga Utsav , along with initiatives like Swachhta Hi Seva and International Day of Yoga , strengthens the cultural connection between communities and rivers through exhibitions, performances, and public dialogues.
  • Community-Based Conservation Networks: Ganga Praharis and Ganga Seva Doots work at the grassroots level to spread awareness, protect biodiversity, promote sustainable practices, and encourage local communities to become guardians of river ecosystems.
  • Innovative Outreach Initiatives: Activities such as the BSF all-women Ganga river rafting expedition promote environmental awareness and create direct engagement between citizens and river conservation efforts.

Conclusion

The future of river conservation lies in combining scientific interventions, sustainable practices, and active community participation. Through Jan Ganga, the Namami Gange Programme has shown that lasting rejuvenation of the Ganga cannot be achieved by government efforts alone. By transforming citizens from passive observers into responsible stakeholders, the initiative paves the way for a more inclusive and sustainable model of river conservation, where communities become long-term guardians of their natural ecosystems.

Water Hyacinth Menace in Vembanad Lake

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source: TH

The State Wetland Authority Kerala (SWAK) is launching a one-month pilot project at Kumarakom, Kerala, to control the spread of invasive water hyacinth ( Eichhornia crassipes) in Vembanad Lake using robotic technology.

  • Government Framework: The project is being implemented under the National Plan for Conservation of Aquatic Ecosystems (NPCA) of the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC).
  • Vembanad Lake: It is a major Ramsar Wetland Site and one of the most important aquatic ecosystems of Kerala, supporting biodiversity, fisheries, tourism, and local livelihoods.

Water Hyacinth ( Eichhornia crassipes )

  • Scientific Identity: Water hyacinth ( Eichhornia crassipes ) is a free-floating freshwater aquatic weed belonging to the Pontederiaceae family and is an invasive alien species.
  • Origin: It is native to the Amazon Basin of South America and was introduced in many countries, including India, as an ornamental plant due to its attractive purple flowers .
  • Rapid Growth and Spread: Water hyacinth reproduces rapidly and forms dense floating mats, blocking sunlight penetration and reducing dissolved oxygen levels, thereby affecting aquatic biodiversity.
  • Impact on Ecosystem: Excessive growth causes eutrophication and chokes rivers, lakes, ponds and wetlands, disrupting fisheries, navigation, irrigation channels, tourism activities and recreational use .
  • Management: Controlled through mechanical removal, biological control using Neochetina weevils, and reduction of nutrient pollution/sewage discharge to prevent excessive growth.
  • Uses: Can be utilised for compost, bio-fertiliser, soil supplements, handicrafts, paper production, and phytoremediation (removal of pollutants from water).

Banks Seek CRR Cut on Green Deposits

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source: ET

In a significant move to accelerate India’s transition towards a net-zero economy, public sector banks have approached the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) seeking a reduction in the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) applicable to green deposits .

  • The Proposal: Banks have advocated for an initial CRR reduction of up to 100 basis points (1%) on green deposits to make green lending cheaper and prioritize it as a preferred credit option.
  • Current Scenario: Despite growth, green deposits raised in 2025-26 were only around Rs 4,000-5,000 crore , which is significantly below the critical mass required to fund India's climate goals.
    • According to the NITI Aayog report 'Scenario towards Viksit Bharat & Net Zero - Financing Needs' , India requires a massive USD 22.7 trillion in cumulative investment to successfully achieve its net-zero transition .
  • Rationale for Cut: A lower CRR would reduce funding costs and free up capital, compensating banks for the higher compliance costs incurred in continuous project evaluation to prevent greenwashing (misuse of green funds).
    • To ease the burden on domestic banks and NBFCs , India must finalize its under-development climate finance taxonomy and create a coordinated regulatory environment to attract long-term global capital .
  • Green Deposits: A green deposit is an interest-bearing deposit received by a regulated entity (such as a bank or an NBFC) for a fixed period.
    • The proceeds from these deposits are earmarked strictly for allocation towards eligible green projects.
    • Eligible Sectors: Under the RBI Green Deposit Framework 2023 , funds must be used for sectors like renewable energy, waste management, clean transportation, climate change adaptation, and energy efficiency .
    • Green Deposits differ from Green Bonds , which are debt securities issued to raise funds for eligible green projects.

Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)

  • Definition: CRR is the percentage of a bank’s Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) that must be maintained as cash reserves with the RBI.
  • Interest: Banks do not earn any interest on the money parked with the RBI under the CRR mandate.
  • Impact on Liquidity: A high CRR drains excess liquidity from the banking system, reducing the banks' lending capacity. Conversely, a lower CRR frees up capital, allowing banks to lend more money to borrowers.

6th World Nomad Games

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source: IE

The Prime Minister of India attended the opening ceremony of the 6 th World Nomad Games in  Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan , highlighting India’s growing cultural and strategic engagement with Central Asia.

  • Origin & Evolution: World Nomad Games established via the 2012 Bishkek Declaration (Kyrgyzstan, Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Turkey) to promote the traditions, history and cultural heritage of nomadic peoples.
    • First held in 2014, the World Nomad Games (WNG) has evolved into a UN-recognised biennial platform for intercultural dialogue and sustainable development, attracting participants from 90+ countries and hosted across regions including Central Asia, Türkiye and Kazakhstan.
  • Core Focus: Distinct from the standard Olympics, the event emphasizes ancestral civilizational heritage over strict geopolitical ties, uniting nations to celebrate traditional nomadic survival practices like horseback riding, falconry, and indigenous wrestling.
  • Three Major Components:
    • Sports Programme: Features traditional athletic and tactical games such as Kok Boru, Kurash, Goresh, Mongol Bokh, archery, and Toguz Korgool.
    • Cultural Programme: Showcases indigenous music, crafts, cuisine, and festivals. It is officially inscribed on the UNESCO Register of Good Safeguarding Practices under the project title "Nomad Games: Rediscovering Heritage, Celebrating Diversity" .
    • Scientific Programme: Dedicated to researching nomadic history, preserving traditional knowledge, and fostering intercultural dialogue.
  • Record Indian Participation (2026): Fielded an 87-member contingent competing in traditional disciplines such as Kurash, Goresh, Mongol Bokh (wrestling), Kok Boru (equestrian), and Toguz Korgool (strategy board games).
    • Kok Boru, a traditional Kyrgyz horseback game similar to polo , recognised by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2017 .
  • Geopolitical Relevance for India: Acts as a cultural diplomacy tool to advance strategic interests in Central Asia, including post- Taliban security cooperation , countering Chinese influence , securing energy needs , and promoting connectivity networks like the International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC) .
