Democratizing AI for Inclusive Growth in India

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

For Prelims: Artificial Intelligence , Digital Public Infrastructure , Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises , India Stack , Bhashini , NPCI , AIRAWAT

For Mains: Role of AI in inclusive development and governance, India’s Digital Public Infrastructure as a global model, AI and socio-economic transformation in the Global South

Source:IE

Why in News?

The global Artificial Intelligence discourse is shifting from infrastructure development to practical applications for inclusive growth. India can lead this transition by leveraging its successful Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) model to democratise AI access and promote equitable economic growth.

Summary

  • Artificial Intelligence can boost India’s economic growth, improve MSME productivity, transform agriculture and healthcare, and generate new jobs. However, job displacement, digital divides and technological monopolies remain key challenges.
  • India must leverage Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) and the IndiaAI Mission to democratise AI access, strengthen domestic computing and research capabilities, develop AI skills, and ensure ethical, affordable and environmentally sustainable AI adoption.

What is the Economic Potential of Artificial Intelligence in India?

  • Massive Value Addition: AI has the potential to add an estimated USD 1.7 trillion to the Indian economy by 2035 by raising capabilities across businesses, households, and governments.
  • Transforming MSMEs: India's "AI-cost geography" favours the deployment of cheap, narrow, and "good-enough" AI.
    • This allows Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) to affordably automate bookkeeping, inventory management, and quality inspection, bridging the productivity gap with large corporations.
  • Emergence of New Job Categories: While AI disrupts traditional roles, it is simultaneously generating entirely new professions in India, such as AI conversation designers, ethical AI specialists, AI trainers, and remote robot operators (e.g., upskilling manual scavengers to operate robotic cleaning systems).
    • According to NITI Aayog, India could create up to 4 million AI jobs by 2031. Strengthening AI skills could position India as the global AI workforce hub, drive innovation and reverse brain drain.
  • Task-Specific Augmentation: Only 4.5% of jobs in developing countries are exposed to total automation.
    • For the vast majority, AI will act as a co-pilot like helping ASHA workers with preliminary health diagnostics (e.g., Wadhwani AI’s tuberculosis-screening tools) and supporting farmers via localized advisories.
    • India has approximately 490 million informal workers, constituting around 90% of its workforce and contributing substantially to GDP. AI can improve their productivity, market access, financial inclusion and livelihood security through personalised advisory services, digital tools and targeted skilling.

What are the Primary AI Economic and Ecological Challenges for India?

  • Job Crisis: As per IIT Madras research, AI automates tasks before it automates entire jobs.
    • However, this "task automation" freezes the entry-level career pipeline. Fresh graduates face shrinking opportunities as AI takes over routine data processing and basic coding, while a thin layer of highly skilled, experienced professionals commands massive wage premiums.
    • NITI Aayog estimates that over 60% of India's formal-sector jobs possess some exposure to AI-driven task changes by 2030.
  • Severe Ecological Footprint: AI data centres are immensely resource-intensive.
    • India’s data centre sector consumes an estimated 150 billion litres of water annually, projected to exceed 358 billion litres by 2030.
    • Alarmingly, over 70% of India's capacity is concentrated in heavily water-stressed urban centres like Chennai, Bengaluru, Mumbai, and Hyderabad.
    • This has triggered local protests over concerns that the AI projects could threaten municipal drinking water supplies and agricultural irrigation.
  • Market Monopolies: The fundamental building blocks of AI advanced chips, large-scale compute, risk capital, and research talent are heavily concentrated in a few Western hyperscalers.
    • This dependence creates geopolitical vulnerabilities and threatens to establish unassailable tech monopolies.
  • The "Complements" Gap: AI's value is entirely dependent on its ecosystem. Without investments in reliable electricity, high-speed connectivity, affordable devices, and local-language datasets, AI will only benefit the urban elite, widening the digital divide.
  • Pilot-to-Scale Gap: Despite the proliferation of AI pilot projects, limited institutional ownership, sustainable financing, interoperability and rigorous impact evaluation can prevent successful innovations from achieving population-scale adoption.

Lessons from India’s DPI Model to Democratise AI Access

India’s experience with the " India Stack" demonstrates that technology need not be a driver of inequality. The DPI approach can be adapted for AI through three core pillars:

  • Technology (Open and Interoperable): Just as UPI provided an open protocol for payments, an AI-DPI must provide open-source foundational models, particularly for regional languages (e.g., Bhashini ), enabling developers to build hyper-local solutions without starting from scratch.
  • Institutions (Public Rails, Private Innovation): Strong institutional frameworks (like the NPCI for payments) are needed.
    • Public institutions can lay down the "AI rails" such as the National Data Governance Framework and public computer infrastructure (e.g., AIRAWAT ) while private players compete to offer consumer-facing innovations.
  • Market Incentives (Low Cost, High Volume): By subsidising the underlying infrastructure and enforcing interoperability, the cost of AI transactions can be driven down to zero or near-zero.
    • This incentivises startups to create high-volume, low-margin business models that serve the bottom of the pyramid in sectors like agriculture, education, and healthcare.

What Measures are Needed for India to Maximise and Democratise AI’s Economic Impact?

  • Resource-Efficient Operations: Approvals must hinge on the regional ecological carrying capacity, making zero-liquid-discharge systems and the use of reclaimed wastewater mandatory to prevent conflicts over municipal and agricultural water supplies.
  • Subsidising Sovereign Compute: The IndiaAI Mission must be rapidly operationalised to provide subsidized Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) access and national computing grids to domestic deep-tech startups and academic institutions.
  • Expanding Sector-Specific AI: The “World Development Report 2026: The Promise of Artificial Intelligence” report recommends an “Adopt, Adapt, Advance” approach, focusing on locally adapted, cost-effective AI rather than expensive frontier models. India must aggressively scale AI-integrated DPIs in critical sectors.
    • In agriculture, the national platform Bharat VISTAAR should deploy local-dialect voice bots for personalised crop and weather advisories, bypassing literacy barriers.
    • Concurrently, to revolutionise rural healthcare, the government must mandate the public procurement of proven domestic AI tools such as Cerebra AI (which achieved 94% TB detection accuracy in Uttar Pradesh) ensuring advanced diagnostics reach the most vulnerable populations.
  • Democratising Data via AI Kosha: AI models are only as good as their training data. The IndiaAI Datasets Platform (AI Kosha) , which currently hosts over 5,500 non-personal public datasets across sectors like agriculture and health, must be expanded to ensure startups can train models without Western data bias.
  • Portable Social Protection: As AI disrupts the labour market, gig and informal workers will face frequent career transitions.
    • Establishing portable social safety net s (health, pension, and unemployment benefits) that move with the worker is essential to absorb economic shocks.
  • Private Capital: The government must expand the Rs 1 lakh crore Deep-Tech Fund of Funds and the Anusandhan National Research Foundation (ANRF) to catalyse private sector investments into domestic R&D, ensuring India becomes an innovator, not just a consumer.
  • Decentralising AI Education: To mitigate the entry-level job freeze. The IndiaAI FutureSkills pillar must accelerate the establishment of Data and AI Labs in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities through ITIs , shifting focus from basic coding to AI literacy and prompt engineering.
  • Targeting Jobs Deficit: Through the MSDE- WEF India Skills Accelerator and targeted research fellowships, India must enact a robust talent-retention framework to reverse brain drain, aiming to capture the 4 million new deep-tech jobs projected by 2031.
  • Safe and Trusted AI Pillar: To prevent algorithmic discrimination and deepfakes, India must operationalise self-assessment checklists and ethical frameworks as envisioned under the IndiaAI Mission, aligning with the proposed Digital India Act.

Conclusion

The transition to an AI-driven economy is inevitable, but its trajectory is a matter of policy choice. India must pivot from being a mere consumer of Western AI models to an active architect of inclusive AI infrastructure. By leveraging its proven DPI playbook, India can ensure that AI acts not as a moat for the elite, but as an escalator for the masses , driving the nation toward sustainable and inclusive growth in the Amrit Kaal.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. What is the role of Digital Public Infrastructure in democratising AI in India?
DPI provides open, interoperable and affordable digital systems that enable wider AI access. Models such as UPI, India Stack and Bhashini offer lessons for inclusive AI deployment.

2. What is the IndiaAI Mission, and how can it support AI development?
The IndiaAI Mission, approved with an outlay of ₹10,371.92 crore over five years in March 2024, aims to strengthen India's AI ecosystem through computing infrastructure, datasets, skills, innovation and responsible AI development.

3. What is AI Kosha, and why is it significant?
AI Kosha is the IndiaAI initiative for facilitating access to datasets and AI resources. It can support the development of locally relevant AI models and reduce dependence on foreign datasets.

4. How can AI contribute to employment generation while addressing job displacement?
AI can create new occupations in AI development, training and governance while augmenting existing jobs. Reskilling, vocational education and stronger social protection are essential to address displacement and entry-level employment challenges.

5. What are the major environmental challenges associated with AI data centres?
AI data centres consume substantial amounts of electricity and water, potentially worsening local resource stress. Renewable energy, reclaimed wastewater, efficient cooling and location-specific environmental assessments can promote sustainable AI infrastructure.

RBI Urges States to Adopt Prudent Borrowing for Fiscal Sustainability

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

For Prelims: Reserve Bank of India , Capital Expenditure , Inflation , Gross Fiscal Deficit , SASCI scheme

For Mains: Centre-State financial relations, Fiscal federalism, Finance Commission recommendations, and State borrowing powers

Source: TH

Why in News?

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Governor, while addressing the 36 th Conference of State Finance Secretaries , urged State governments to adopt prudent borrowing strategies and efficient cash management practices to maintain their long-term fiscal health.

Summary

  • Indian States face rising debt-to-GSDP ratios, fiscal deficits, off-budget borrowings and high committed expenditure, which reduce funds available for developmental activities and threaten long-term fiscal sustainability.
  • States must adopt prudent borrowing, efficient cash management and FRBM compliance, prioritise capital expenditure, improve transparency in public finances and strengthen fiscal oversight to ensure sustainable growth and intergenerational equity.

What is Prudent Borrowing?

  • About: Prudent borrowing refers to a fiscal management strategy where a government limits its debt intake strictly to its long-term repayment capacity, ensuring that the borrowed funds are utilized efficiently.
    • A core tenet of prudent borrowing is the "Golden Rule of Public Finance" borrowing should primarily be directed toward Capital Expenditure (Capex) (infrastructure, health, and education facilities) that generates future revenue, rather than Revenue Expenditure (day-to-day administrative costs or non-merit subsidies).
  • Effective Cash Management: Prudent Borrowing involves aligning the borrowing calendar with actual cash-flow mismatches , preventing the accumulation of idle cash balances that incur unnecessary interest costs.

Significance of Prudent Borrowing

  • Macroeconomic Stability: It prevents the "crowding out" effect , ensuring that the government does not absorb the entire domestic credit pool, thereby leaving adequate and affordable credit for the private sector.
  • Fiscal Space for Welfare: A large debt stock leads to massive interest payments. Currently, a significant portion of the Union and State revenue receipts is consumed merely by servicing debt.
    • Lower debt through Prudent Borrowing lowers interest payment obligations, freeing up the budget (fiscal space) for critical developmental sectors like public health, green transitions, and education.
  • Vulnerability to Sovereign Downgrades: High debt-to-GDP ratios keep sovereign credit ratings in the lower investment grades (e.g., BBB-), making external commercial borrowings expensive for Indian corporates.
  • Inflationary Pressures: Unchecked fiscal deficits, if monetised, or if they drive up aggregate demand without commensurate supply , can lead to persistent structural inflation.
  • Inter-Generational Equity: Prudent borrowing ensures that current consumption is not financed by imposing an unsustainable tax burden on future generations.

What is the Current State of Fiscal Health of Indian States?

While the consolidated General Government Debt (Centre + States) hovers around 81- 85% of GDP , state government debt accounts for a massive one-third of this overall figure. The fiscal health across states is highly asymmetric, characterised by the following trends:

  • Elevated and Divergent Debt-to-GSDP Ratios: Aggregate state debt stood at 29.2% of GDP at the end of March 2026 , remaining significantly above the 20% prudence limit recommended by the FRBM Review Committee.
    • Highly Stressed States: States like Punjab and West Bengal struggle with severe fiscal stress, carrying debt burdens near or above 40% of their GSDP .
    • The Prudent Performers: Conversely, Gujarat, Maharashtra, and Odisha have successfully contained their debt-to-GSDP ratios within the 20% limit.
    • Consolidation Efforts: States such as Kerala (35.5%) and Tamil Nadu (29.2%) have shown fiscal discipline, reducing their outstanding liabilities by nearly 4 to 5 percentage points since 2021.
  • Breaching Fiscal Deficit Targets: The states' consolidated Gross Fiscal Deficit (GFD) has widened and is budgeted at 3.3% of GDP for FY 2025-26 .
    • Alarmingly, 16 states budgeted a GFD exceeding the standard 3% limit, driven largely by a decline in central grants and revenue shortfalls.
  • Rising Cost of Borrowing & Expenditure Rigidity: The sharp rise in government bond yields in October 2026 threatens to inflate the cost of financing state deficits.
    • Furthermore, states are trapped by high committed expenditures collectively spending over 60% of their revenue receipts strictly on interest payments, salaries, pensions, and subsidies. This leaves minimal fiscal space for discretionary developmental projects.
  • Crucial Role in the Economy: States account for two-thirds of total public spending and one-third of total revenue in India.
    • Their fiscal performance, particularly their capacity to sustain Capital Expenditure (Capex) currently heavily supported by the Centre's 50-year interest-free loans under the SASCI scheme is central to the nation's long-term developmental trajectory.

What are the Key Issues in Current Borrowing Practices of States?

  • Off-Budget Borrowings: States frequently bypass the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) borrowing limits by raising funds through State Public Sector Enterprises (SPSEs) or Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs). These loans are often backed by state government guarantees, circumventing legislative scrutiny.
  • Revenue vs. Capital Expenditure: Borrowing is economically justified if utilized for Capex , which creates income-generating assets and has a high fiscal multiplier (approx. 2.45).
    • However, states increasingly borrow to fund Revenue Expenditure (subsidies, loan waivers, and freebies), which yields no or less future returns.
  • Contingent Liabilities: State guarantees to DISCOMs (power distribution companies) remain a ticking fiscal time bomb, threatening to devolve onto state budgets if these entities default.
  • Market Volatility: Uncalibrated borrowing calendars by the Centre and States can lead to sudden spikes in bond yields , disrupting the bond market and monetary policy transmission.

What Measures are Needed for Sustaining Long-Term Fiscal Health of States?

  • Leveraging Digital Financial Platforms: As advised by the RBI Governor, states must onboard onto the Unified Lending Interface (ULI) and utilize Programmable CBDC (P-CBDC) for Direct Benefit Transfers.
    • Programmable CBDCs can ensure that welfare funds are strictly utilized for their intended purpose, plugging leakages and enhancing the efficiency of public spending.
  • Effective Use of the SLBC Forum: The State Level Bankers’ Committee (SLBC) is a vital inter-institutional forum for coordinating banking and development.
    • States must actively use SLBCs to monitor credit disbursement, resolve bottlenecks in priority sector lending, and improve rural infrastructure financing, thereby stimulating local economic growth and increasing state revenues.
  • Strict Adherence to FRBM Targets: States must strictly adhere to the fiscal deficit glide path mandated by their respective State Fiscal Responsibility Legislations (typically capped at 3 to 3.5% of GSDP).
  • Establishing an Independent Fiscal Council: As recommended by the 13th and 14th Finance Commissions and the N.K. Singh Committee (2016), an independent Fiscal Council should be established to assess government forecasts, ensure debt sustainability, and bring transparency to off-budget financing.
  • Capping Off-Budget Borrowings: The RBI and the Ministry of Finance must enforce strict accounting standards.
    • The move by the Union Government to equate state off-budget borrowings with their standard borrowing limits under Article 293(3) is a positive step .
  • Dynamic Debt Management: States should adopt the practices outlined in the RBI's 'Compendium on Cash and Debt Management' .
    • This includes aligning market borrowings accurately with expenditure needs and adopting the Benchmark Issuance Strategy to improve liquidity and yield curves.

Conclusion

Prudent borrowing is not synonymous with zero borrowing; rather, it is about maintaining a delicate balance between leveraging debt for productive capital formation and avoiding the debt trap of consumption-led borrowing. As India navigates the Amrit Kaal towards becoming a developed nation, institutionalizing fiscal discipline and inter-generational equity will be the bedrock of its macroeconomic resilience.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. What is prudent borrowing, and why is it important for State governments?
Prudent borrowing aligns public debt with repayment capacity and prioritises productive capital expenditure over routine consumption, supporting debt sustainability and intergenerational equity.

2. What does Article 293 of the Indian Constitution provide?
Article 293 governs borrowing by States. Under Article 293(3), a State indebted to the Union requires the Union Government's consent to raise further loans, subject to the constitutional conditions.

3. What is the FRBM framework, and how does it promote fiscal discipline?
The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) framework seeks to promote fiscal stability through deficit and debt management, greater transparency and responsible government borrowing.

4. What are off-budget borrowings, and why are they a concern?
Off-budget borrowings are liabilities raised through public entities or other arrangements outside the regular State budget. They can conceal the true debt burden and weaken fiscal transparency.

5. What is the SASCI scheme, and how does it support State finances?
The Scheme for Special Assistance to States for Capital Investment (SASCI) provides long-term interest-free loans to States for capital investment, supporting infrastructure creation and economic growth.

Decoding the Genetics of Personality

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source:TH

A study published in Nature revealed that there is no single ‘personality gene’ , establishing that while personality has a biological basis, individual genetic variations have a minuscule effect compared to environmental factors.

  • The ‘Big Five’ Framework: The research analyzed the genetic architecture of the five broad buckets of human psychology: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism .
  • No Single 'Personality Gene': Utilizing a Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) across 1.14 million participants, researchers identified roughly 1,250 tiny DNA variations associated with personality traits .
    • This proves that behavioral traits are a complex phenotype governed by patterns spread across the genome, rather than a single gene or particular brain chemicals.
  • The Polygenic Index and Health Outcomes: Researchers tallied these gene variants into a polygenic index .
    • They found that genetic patterns associated with certain traits correlate with health and social outcomes (e.g., higher conscientiousness is linked to lower body mass index; neuroticism is linked to psychiatric disorders).
  • Nature vs. Nurture: By studying siblings with shared environments but different DNA, researchers isolated the genetic impact.
    • The findings revealed that the environmental transfer of personality from parents to children is neither strong nor straightforward .
  • Macroscopic vs. Individual Utility: For an individual, genetic sequencing provides less predictive value about personality. However, the genetic data is highly valuable at the macroscopic level for population-based psychological, biological, and social sciences .
  • Limitations: A major bottleneck in applying this research globally is that current genomic databases are heavily dominated by individuals with European-like genomes , making predictions less reliable for diverse genetic ancestries.
    • To overcome global data bottlenecks, experts suggest that DNA sequencing within families exhibiting extreme behavioral variants is the way forward.
    • In India, this approach is already being pioneered for psychiatric conditions through joint efforts by the National Centre for Biological Sciences (NCBS) and the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS) in Bengaluru.

Exercise Garuda Shakti 2026

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source: PIB

The 11th edition of the India-Indonesia Joint Special Forces Exercise GARUDA SHAKTI commenced at Banyuwangi, Indonesia .

  • Participating Forces: The Indian contingent comprises personnel from the Parachute Regiment (Special Forces) of the Indian Army , while the Indonesian contingent includes personnel from the Indonesian Special Forces.
  • Objective: Strengthen mutual understanding, operational coordination and interoperability between the Special Forces of India and Indonesia.
  • Training Activities: Jungle warfare tactics, obstacle crossing, combat marksmanship, mountaineering, air assault training, combat-hardening sessions and exchange of expertise on weapons, equipment and operational practices.
  • Validation Exercise: The joint training will conclude with a simulated operational exercise to assess the endurance, coordination and combat readiness of both contingents.
  • Significance: Strengthens India-Indonesia defence cooperation, promotes military interoperability and reinforces bilateral security ties in the Indo-Pacific region.
  • Other Exercises: Samudra Shakti (bilateral naval exercise), IND-INDO CORPAT (coordinated naval patrol).

India’s Core Sector Records Strong Growth

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source: TH

India’s core industrial growth rose to 7.9% in September 2026 , the highest under the new series since at least April 2024.

  • Major Drivers: Growth was led by iron ore (18.4%) , electricity (11.4%) , coal (10.7%) and refinery products (10.3%) .
  • About Core Industries: The Index of Core Industries (ICI) tracks key infrastructure sectors and serves as an important indicator of overall industrial performance.
    • The Index of Eight Core Industries (ICI) is compiled monthly by the O ffice of the Economic Adviser (OEA), under the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT), Ministry of Commerce and Industry.
      • ICI is compiled on a monthly basis and the objective is to track the performance of key infrastructure industries.
    • The base year of the Index of Core Industries (ICI) has been revised from 2011–12 to 2022–23 and Iron Ore has been included as the 9th core sector (following Praveen Mahto Committee’s recommendations).
    • The 9 core industries are:
      • Petroleum Refinery Products
      • Electricity
      • Steel
      • Coal
      • Crude Oil
      • Natural Gas
      • Cement
      • Fertilizers
      • Iron Ore
