India’s Accession to Southern Indian Ocean Fisheries Agreement

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

Source: PIB

Why in News?

The Central Government approved India's accession to the Southern Indian Ocean Fisheries Agreement (SIOFA). This decision elevates India’s status from a Cooperating Non-Contracting Party (CNCP) to a full Contracting Party, marking a strategic milestone in India's role in global fisheries governance.

What is the Southern Indian Ocean Fisheries Agreement (SIOFA)?

  • About: SIOFA is a multilateral regional fisheries agreement and Regional Fisheries Management Organisation (RFMO) aimed at ensuring the long-term conservation and sustainable use of fishery resources in the high seas of the Southern Indian Ocean.
  • Origin & Enforcement: The agreement was adopted in July 2006 at the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) headquarters in Rome and entered into force in June 2012 and became fully operational in 2016.
    • It was established following the abolition of the Indian Ocean Fisheries Commission (IOFC) in 1999. SIOFA headquarters is located in Le Port, Reunion Island.
  • Core Mandate: Its primary objective is to manage fishery resources on the high seas through multilateral cooperation, specifically taking into account the developmental needs of developing States and Small-Island Developing States (SIDS) bordering the area.
  • Species Coverage: SIOFA actively manages and assesses resources including fish, molluscs, crustaceans, and other sedentary species .
  • Key Exclusions: The agreement strictly excludes highly migratory species (which are listed in Annex I of UNCLOS , such as tuna) and sedentary species subject to the fishery jurisdiction of coastal states (as defined under Article 77(4) of the 1982 UNCLOS ).
  • Conservation & Management Measures: In 2016, SIOFA established robust regulatory measures, including Vessel Monitoring Systems (VMS) requirements, regulations on bottom fishing to protect Vulnerable Marine Ecosystems (VMEs) , and a stringent listing system to combat Illegal, Unreported, and Unregulated (IUU) fishing .

Key Implications of India's Accession

  • Enhanced Decision-Making Power: India has held Cooperating Non-Contracting Party (CNCP) status since 2022, which limited its role in decision-making. Accession grants India a full seat on the Scientific Committee and the Compliance Committee, providing direct access to scientific data, research programs, and stock assessments.
  • Expansion of the High-Seas Fleet: Currently, Indian fishing vessels operate largely within 60 to 80 nautical miles of its Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) .
    • Full membership allows Indian-flagged vessels and enterprises to undertake authorized commercial fishing in the vast SIOFA jurisdiction.
  • Security and Compliance: It enables India to actively participate in the High Seas Boarding and Inspection (HSBI) Scheme adopted under SIOFA.
    • This strengthens national coastal security and helps prevent Illegal, Unreported, and Unregulated (IUU) fishing.
  • Alignment with National Policy: The accession operationalizes the Guidelines for High Sea Fisheries issued by the Indian government in December 2025, which provided the policy framework to expand deep-sea fishing operations and boost the Blue Economy.
  • Geopolitical Counterbalance: The High Seas constitute roughly 65% of the global ocean and are currently dominated by fleets from China (21%) , Taiwan, Japan, South Korea, and Spain. India's accession secures its legitimate rights and establishes a recognized presence to counterbalance foreign dominance in the Southern Indian Ocean.

Note: SIOFA is one of two major Regional Fisheries Management Organisations (RFMOs) in the Indian Ocean. The other is the Indian Ocean Tuna Commission (IOTC) .

  • SIOFA differs from IOTC in its mandate, as SIOFA manages non-highly migratory fish stocks in the Southern Indian Ocean, while IOTC regulates highly migratory tuna and tuna-like species across the Indian Ocean. India has been a Contracting Party to IOTC since 1996.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. What is the Southern Indian Ocean Fisheries Agreement (SIOFA)?
SIOFA is a multilateral fisheries agreement adopted in 2006 and operational since 2012, aimed at conserving and sustainably managing fishery resources in the high seas of the Southern Indian Ocean.

2. What is the significance of India's accession to SIOFA?
It strengthens India's role in fisheries decision-making, scientific cooperation, high-seas fishing and efforts to combat Illegal, Unreported and Unregulated (IUU) fishing.

3. Which marine species fall under SIOFA's mandate?
SIOFA covers specified fish, molluscs, crustaceans and other sedentary species in its area of competence, excluding highly migratory species such as tuna.

4. How does SIOFA differ from the Indian Ocean Tuna Commission (IOTC)?
IOFA manages covered fishery resources in the Southern Indian Ocean high seas, while IOTC manages tuna and tuna-like species in the Indian Ocean. India has been an IOTC Contracting Party since 1996.

5. What is the role of UNCLOS in high-seas fisheries governance?
The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), 1982, establishes the international legal framework for ocean use, including high-seas freedoms, conservation obligations and coastal-state rights over resources within their maritime jurisdiction.

Fair International Law: Navigating the Authoritarian Challenge

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

For Prelims: 2026 Nobel Peace Prize , United Nations Charter , International Court of Justice , International Civil Aviation Organization , International Telecommunication Union , UNCLOS , High Seas Treaty , Montreal Protocol

For Mains: Multilateralism in a multipolar world is both a necessity and a challenge, International institutions, UN reforms, International law.

Source: TH

Why in News?

The awarding of the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize to South African jurist Navi Pillay (former UN High Commissioner for Human Rights) has arrived at a critical geopolitical juncture. Amidst the ongoing wars in Ukraine and West Asia , and the brazen violation of the United Nations Charter , international law is facing an existential crisis.

  • However, the Nobel Committee’s recognition serves as a definitive global endorsement that a fair, rights-based international legal order remains indispensable in countering the aggressive rise of "Authoritarian International Law."

Summary

  • International law is facing a crisis due to the rise of Authoritarian International Law, selective compliance by powerful states, rejection of international judicial rulings and the erosion of universal legal norms.
  • A fair international legal order requires stronger multilateral institutions, equitable global representation, consistent enforcement of international law and India's leadership in promoting reformed multilateralism.

What is International Law?

  • Definition: International law is the body of legal rules, norms, and standards that apply between sovereign states and other entities legally recognized as international actors.
  • Core Objective: It seeks to maintain international peace and security, primarily codified by Article 2(4) of the UN Charter , which explicitly prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state.
  • Sources of Law: As per Article 38 of the International Court of Justice (ICJ) Statute , International Law is derived from international conventions (treaties), international custom (state practice), general principles of law, and judicial decisions.
  • The Subject of Law: The post-WW II liberal order fundamentally shifted international law to make the individual a subject of law, institutionalizing civil, political, and human rights.

Current Status

  • A "Discipline of Crisis": As described by ICJ, international law is currently operating in a crisis mode, battered by imperialistic designs and a precipitous decline in human rights compliance.
  • The "Silent Efficacy": While the breaches of the UN Charter regarding the "use of force" dominate media headlines, the vast majority of international law operates silently, seamlessly, and with near-universal compliance every single day . This "functional" international law sustains global interdependence across multiple domains:
    • Global Connectivity: The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) quietly coordinate thousands of daily cross-border flights and global internet/ GPS spectrums without dispute.
    • Maritime & Global Commons: UNCLOS peacefully regulates 90% of global shipping trade, complemented by the recent High Seas Treaty (BBNJ) and the Outer Space Treaty , which helps prevent  the militarisation of space.
    • Ecological Triumphs: The Montreal Protocol stands as a historic success, actively healing the ozone layer through universally enforced phase-outs.
    • Routine Transnational Governance: Agencies like WIPO (patents/IP), Universal Postal Union (cross-border postal delivery), and the WHO’s International Health Regulations manage daily global cooperation, even among bitter diplomatic rivals.

How are States 'Retooling' International Law?

  • Weaponizing State Sovereignty: While developing nations historically invoked sovereignty to resist colonialism, regimes now use it as an absolute shield against international scrutiny.
    • China routinely dismisses UN OHCHR reports regarding human rights in Xinjiang as "illegal interference in internal affairs."
    • Similarly, Israel systematically rejects resolutions and reports by UN Human Rights bodies regarding Gaza and the West Bank on the grounds of sovereign interference.
    • The US has withdrawn from international frameworks such as the Paris Agreement and UNESCO, illustrating how major powers can disengage from multilateral institutions when they conflict with domestic priorities.
  • Creating Pliant, Weak International Organizations (IOs): Instead of joining IOs with independent regulatory or supranational authority (like the EU or early UN), authoritarian states prefer establishing forums where member-states retain absolute control and the IO cannot enforce accountability.
    • The US-backed Board of Peace for Gaza has raised concerns about creating alternative global mechanisms outside established UN structures. This, alongside the US veto of a Gaza ceasefire resolution in June 2025 , highlights concerns about the selective use of multilateral institutions.
    • The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) , prioritizes regime security and "non-interference" over human rights or democratic accountability, possessing no independent judicial body to hold its member states accountable.
  • Rejecting third-party adjudication: There is a growing global backlash against independent international courts, with powerful states increasingly preferring opaque bilateral negotiations where they can exploit their military and economic advantages.
    • For instance, China rejected the 2016 Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA) ruling in favour of the Philippines in the South China Sea dispute.
    • Similarly, the US has historically opposed the International Criminal Court (ICC) and imposed economic sanctions in 2025 targeting ICC officials over investigations into alleged war crimes , undermining the independence of international judicial institutions.
  • Securitization of Economic Law (Protectionism): Major hegemonic powers are dismantling the neoliberal economic consensus, violating free-trade norms under the guise of "national security."
    • For instance, the US blocking of appointments to the WTO’s Appellate Body , effectively paralyzing global trade dispute resolution.
    • Additionally, the US Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) and CHIPS Act , which offer massive domestic industrial subsidies that arguably violate WTO non-discrimination principles, are justified as national security imperatives against China.
  • "Rules-Based Order": Democratic powers have contributed to this retooling by replacing the binding term "International Law" (which requires strict treaty compliance) with the politically ambiguous phrase "Rules-Based Order."
    • Western nations frequently invoke the "rules-based order" to condemn Russia's invasion of Ukraine or China's maritime incursions, but bypass the UN Security Council for their own military interventions (e.g., the 2003 Iraq War or NATO's intervention in Libya/Kosovo ).
    • This allows powerful nations to selectively apply rules to adversaries while exempting themselves.

Implications

Global

  • Erosion of Jus Cogens Norms: Peremptory norms like the prohibition of genocide, territorial conquest, and war crimes are being systematically undermined, creating a dangerous precedent for future state conduct.
  • Credibility Deficit: The selective application of international law where the Global North demands compliance from adversaries but shields its allies is eroding the legitimacy of multilateral institutions across the Global South.
  • Return of "Spheres of Influence": A shift away from universal law invites a fragmented world order governed by neo-imperialism and raw military might.

India

  • The Maritime Security Dilemma: India heavily relies on a universally respected UNCLOS to maintain a free and open Indo-Pacific and counter Chinese expansionism.
    • The weakening of international law directly threatens India's maritime security.
  • Trade and Economic Vulnerability: As a rapidly growing developing economy, India requires a functional, rules-based multilateral trading system.
    • The paralysis of the WTO Dispute Settlement Mechanism leaves India vulnerable to unilateral protectionist tariffs from major powers.
  • Strategic Balancing: India faces the complex task of navigating between a hypocritical Western "rules-based order" and the Sino-Russian authoritarian retooling, requiring New Delhi to champion strict adherence to the UN Charter while protecting its strategic autonomy.
  • Commitment to Multilateralism: India has consistently advocated reforms in global institutions while upholding the existing international order, demonstrated through its contributions to UN peacekeeping , participation in international judicial bodies and acceptance of negotiated legal settlements.

Authoritarian International Law

Coined by legal scholars like Tom Ginsburg, Authoritarian International Law refers to a framework where international legal norms are reinterpreted to prioritize regime survival, executive power, and state sovereignty over individual rights and democratic accountability.

  • Contrast with Liberal Order: The traditional liberal international law (established post-1945) views the individual as a subject of international law, emphasizing human dignity, fairness, and accountability.
    • Authoritarian international law, conversely, seeks to shield governments from external scrutiny.
  • Core Philosophy: It views international law purely as a tool for coordinating regime survival , rejecting the idea that international law should protect civil liberties or hold executive power accountable.

What are the Challenges in Preserving a Fair International Law?

  • The Enforcement Deficit: Unlike domestic law, international law lacks a global police force. Courts like the ICJ rely on the UN Security Council (UNSC) for enforcement, which is frequently paralyzed by the veto power of the P5 nations.
  • Populist Nationalism: Ethno-nationalist regimes globally frame international law as "foreign interference," attacking international institutions to appease domestic political constituencies.
  • Outdated Global Architecture: The Bretton Woods and UN systems reflect the geopolitical realities of 1945.
    • The continued marginalization of Africa, Latin America, and India in global decision-making reduces the universal legitimacy of international law.
  • Normative Interaction vs. Immediate Compliance: A major conceptual challenge is judging international law solely by immediate compliance.
    • Scholars like Harold Hongju Koh argue that its true efficacy lies in Transnational Legal Processes (the gradual internalization of global norms by domestic systems) which is currently being stunted by nationalist pushback.

What Measures are Needed to Preserve International Law?

  • Address the Enforcement Deficit (UNSC Reform): Adopt the French-Mexican initiative demanding the P5 nations voluntarily suspend their veto power in cases of genocide, mass atrocities, and jus cogens violations.
    • Urgent structural reform of the UN Security Council is required, including the expansion of permanent membership (incorporating the G4).
    • When the UNSC is paralyzed, it aggressively invoke Resolution 377 (Uniting for Peace) in the UN General Assembly to recommend collective international action.
  • Reclaim the Legal Vocabulary: Middle powers (like India and Brazil) must reject the Western-coined, hypocritical "Rules-Based Order" and demand a return to strict, universally applied "Binding International Law" based on the UN Charter.
  • Bypass Sabotaged Courts: Expand the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA) to ensure trade disputes are resolved legally despite the US blockade of the WTO Appellate Body.
    • Pass UNGA resolutions forbidding unilateral sanctions or political intimidation against ICC and ICJ judges.
  • Localize Enforcement via 'Universal Jurisdiction': Empower domestic courts to prosecute severe international crimes (crimes against humanity) regardless of where they occurred, similar to Germany’s prosecution of Syrian war criminals , ensuring accountability when global bodies are blocked.
  • Codify Laws for 'Grey Zone' Domains: Draft binding multilateral treaties for emerging domains to prevent "might makes right" tactics.
    • This includes codifying cyber warfare limits (building on the Tallinn Manual ) and updating the 1967 Outer Space Treaty to ban anti-satellite (ASAT) weapons.
  • Reform the Global Financial Architecture: Implement immediate quota reforms in the IMF and World Bank to give the Global South equitable voting rights, preventing the unilateral weaponization of global financial systems (like SWIFT) for economic coercion.
  • Leverage Middle-Power Diplomacy (The G20 Model): India’s successful G20 Presidency (G20 Leaders' New Delhi Declaration) proved that middle powers can bridge the divide between a fractured West and the Sino-Russian bloc, offering a template for consensus-building in polarized times.

Conclusion

As a champion of strategic autonomy and the voice of the Global South, India is uniquely positioned to act as a 'Vishwa Mitra' (Global Friend). By pushing for reformed multilateralism, New Delhi can help build an international legal order that is equitable, universally binding, and free from both Western hypocrisy and authoritarian manipulation.

Frequently asked questions (FAQs)

1. What is international law, and what are its principal sources?
International law governs relations among states and other recognised international actors. Under Article 38 of the ICJ Statute, its principal sources include treaties, international custom and general principles of law, with judicial decisions and scholarly writings serving as subsidiary means.

2. What is Authoritarian International Law?
It refers to the use or reinterpretation of international legal norms to prioritise regime survival, executive power and state sovereignty over individual rights, democratic accountability and external scrutiny.

3. What are the major challenges in preserving a fair international legal order?
Key challenges include the enforcement deficit, selective compliance by powerful states, populist nationalism, outdated global institutions and the unequal representation of developing countries in international decision-making.

4. What measures can strengthen international law and global accountability?
Key measures include UNSC reform, restraint in the use of vetoes during mass atrocities, stronger international dispute settlement, universal jurisdiction, equitable representation of the Global South and clearer rules for emerging technological domains.

Pradhan Mantri Samajik Sahayata Yojana

★ ★ ★ ☆ ☆

Source: PIB

Why in News?

Recently, the Union Cabinet approved the continuation and renaming of the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) as the Pradhan Mantri Samajik Sahayata Yojana , along with revisions to the disability pension.

What are the Key Facts About Pradhan Mantri Samajik Sahayata Yojana?

  • About: Originally launched as the National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP) on 15th August 1995 , it provides financial assistance to Below Poverty Line (BPL) households facing old age, widowhood, disability or the death of a primary breadwinner.
  • Constitutional Basis: Gives effect to Article 41 under the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP), which directs the State to provide public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement, within its economic capacity.
  • Nodal Ministry and Funding: Implemented under the Ministry of Rural Development as a 100% centrally funded Centrally Sponsored Scheme , providing minimum financial assistance to eligible beneficiaries in rural and urban areas .
  • Implementation: Implemented by States and Union Territories, with Gram Panchayats and municipalities assisting in beneficiary identification and payments largely facilitated through Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT).
  • Financial Outlay: Approved allocation of ₹53,428 crore for five years (2026–2031), covering approximately 3.20 crore beneficiaries across all 36 States and UTs.
  • State Supplementation: States and UTs provide additional pension assistance above the Central contribution, with top-ups ranging from ₹50 to ₹5,700 per month (September 2026), resulting in average monthly pensions of around ₹1,100 in several States/UTs.
  • Continuity: The old-age pension, widow pension and family benefit schemes continue unchanged, while the disability pension has been revised in terms of eligibility, coverage and assistance rates.

Revised Components

The programme comprises three monthly pension schemes and one lump-sum family benefit scheme , all renamed under the revised framework:

Significance

  • Inclusive Social Protection: Extends financial assistance to persons with 40–79% disabilities, reducing exclusion under the earlier 80% eligibility threshold .
  • Childhood Disability Support: Removal of the minimum age requirement enables early financial assistance to eligible children with disabilities from BPL households.
  • Financial Security: Enhanced pension rates strengthen basic income support for economically vulnerable persons with disabilities.
  • Cooperative Federalism: Central assistance supplemented by State and UT contributions enables locally responsive social protection, while maintaining a common national minimum.

What are the Key Reforms under Pradhan Mantri Divyangjan Pension Yojana?

  • Background: Introduced in February 2009 as the Indira Gandhi National Disability Pension Scheme, it originally provided pensions to Below Poverty Line (BPL) persons aged 18 years and above with at least 80% disability.
  • Legal Basis: The revisions align the scheme with the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 , enacted to give effect to the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD) .
    • Section 2(r) of the Act defines a person with a benchmark disability as someone with at least 40% of a specified disability, as certified by the competent authority.
  • Revised Eligibility: The minimum disability requirement has been reduced from 80% to 40%, while the minimum age of 18 years has been removed, extending eligibility from birth.
  • Enhanced Pension: Monthly Central assistance has been increased by 25%:
    • Below 80 years: Increased from ₹300 to ₹375 per month.
    • 80 years and above: Increased from ₹500 to ₹625 per month.
  • Expanded Coverage: The number of beneficiaries has been increased from 8.33 lakh to 27.48 lakh.
  • Implementation: Revised guidelines covering eligibility, beneficiary numbers and assistance rates are to be issued and implemented within three months of the Cabinet's approval.

Conclusion

The Pradhan Mantri Samajik Sahayata Yojana strengthens India's social security framework through expanded disability coverage, enhanced financial assistance and continued support for vulnerable households. Aligning disability pensions with the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 represents an important step towards inclusive, rights-based and accessible social protection.

Source: IE

Chief Justice of India (CJI) inaugurated the permanent Ladakh Bench of the Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh High Court in Leh, at an altitude of approximately 3,500 metres , making it India's highest-altitude High Court bench .

  • Establishment: The bench was established under the Union Territory of Ladakh (Sitting of Bench of the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir, and Ladakh in Ladakh) Regulation, 2026, promulgated by the President under Article 240 of the Constitution and Section 58(2) of the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 .

  • Significance: Strengthens access to justice, judicial decentralisation and affordable legal services in the geographically remote Himalayan region, while encouraging greater participation of Ladakh's youth in the legal profession.

IISER Develops Solid Material for Hydrogen and Methane Production

★ ★ ☆ ☆ ☆

Source: TH

Researchers at the Indian Institute of Science Education and Research (IISER), Thiruvananthapuram , developed a solid compound that releases hydrogen and methane when heated.

  • The compound, called magnesium hydridomethylzincate , is made of magnesium, zinc, hydrogen and carbon and remains solid at room temperature.
    • Why It Matters: Hydrogen is difficult and costly to store because of its very low density . A solid material could provide an alternative to transporting fuel gases in compressed form .
    • Limitation: The material is highly sensitive to moisture , and researchers have not yet shown whether it can be recharged with gases after use .
  • About Methane: Methane is the simplest hydrocarbon and the first member of the alkane series, consisting of one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms.
    • It is a colourless, odourless, highly flammable gas , lighter than air, and is the main constituent of natural gas.
    • It is found in underground natural gas deposits and is produced through the decomposition of organic matter in oxygen-deficient environments.
    • It is widely used as a fuel for cooking, heating and electricity generation and as a raw material for producing hydrogen, methanol and other chemicals.
  • About Hydrogen: Hydrogen is the lightest and most abundant chemical element in the universe.
    • It is a colourless, odourless, tasteless and highly flammable gas .
    • It is found in water (H₂O) , organic compounds and stars, but rarely occurs in its free form on Earth.
    • It is used in ammonia production , petroleum refining, rocket fuel and hydrogen fuel cells.
    • Green Hydrogen is produced by splitting water through electrolysis using renewable energy sources, it offers a low-emission alternative to hydrogen produced from fossil fuels.
